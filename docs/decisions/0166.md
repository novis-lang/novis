# ADR 0166 — Every decompression runs under two numbers, and neither has a spelling for "off"

- **Scope:** the bound `Core\Compress` and `Core\Zip` share — what it is made of, where its default
  comes from, which direction a call may move it, and what a breach is. It decides nothing about
  either class's surface, which is [0051](0051.md) § 3's roster row and
  [01 § 17](../spec/01-core-library.md)'s, and nothing about the archive-entry policy, which is
  `Core\Zip`'s own.
- **Depends on:** [0051](0051.md), which puts both classes at Tier 0 *because* this is policy;
  [0097](0097.md) § 1, which drops response compression at the edge and leaves the decompression
  budget to the request; [0004](0004.md), whose ordering is what spends a request's memory on a
  refusal it can survive; [0064](0064.md), whose `[limits]` block carries the ceiling.
- **Validated by:** `crates/nvs-stdlib/src/compress.rs`'s six bound cases and
  `tests/conformance/core/compress-refuses-a-decompression-past-its-bound.nvst`.

> **In short:** a decompression is bounded by an **absolute output ceiling** and a **ratio of output
> to input**, together, because neither is a bound on its own. `[limits] max_decompressed` and
> `[limits] max_decompression_ratio` are the operator's, `System`-class and defaulting to 64 MiB and
> 1000:1; a call may ask for less through `$maxBytes` and `$maxRatio` and gets the smaller of the two
> on each axis. There is no argument, and no configuration, that means "no bound" — `false`, which
> `rule:config/three-changeability-classes` spells "no ceiling" for the limits a request may raise,
> reads here as the shipped default. A breach is a `ParseError` naming the rule, thrown rather than
> answered as a truncation.

## Context

### 1. What PHP shipped, and what it cost

PHP's decompressors take `$max_length`, it is optional, and it defaults to unlimited. Every one of
the nine spellings the `zlib` extension carries — `gzuncompress`, `gzinflate`, `gzdecode`,
`zlib_decode` and their handle-shaped relatives — inherits that default, so the safe call is the one
the caller has to remember to write. `ZipArchive` does not offer even that: an entry's uncompressed
size is read from a header the archive itself supplies, which is a number the attacker wrote.

The failure mode is not subtle and is not rare. A 42 KB archive that expands to 4.5 PB has been
public for over a decade; the interesting ones are far smaller and far more ordinary — a request
body under `Content-Encoding: gzip`, a few kilobytes on the wire, unpacking into more memory than
the process has. What makes it a *policy* question rather than a library one is that the caller who
forgets is the caller who most needed the default: the program handling an upload endpoint.

### 2. Why the bound is two numbers

A single number is a bound with a hole in it, and which hole depends on which number.

An **absolute ceiling alone** — "no decompression may produce more than 64 MiB" — is a ceiling every
request may reach. A 40-octet frame and a 40-megabyte upload are entitled to the same output, so a
handler that decompresses one small field per request has quietly authorised 64 MiB per request for
a body that could not honestly produce a hundredth of it. The ceiling stops the catastrophic case
and leaves the amplification untouched.

A **ratio alone** — "no more than 1000 octets out per octet in" — has the opposite hole. It is
proportionate, which is exactly its problem: a large input buys a proportionately large output, so
an attacker who can upload 100 MB can ask for 100 GB, and every step of it is within the rule.

Together they are the bound each is missing: `min(input × ratio, ceiling)`, computed per call, with
the ratio deciding for small inputs and the ceiling for large ones. That is one comparison in the
decode loop, and it is the whole enforcement.

## Investigation

The two numbers were chosen against what real inputs do, not against what would be comfortable.

**64 MiB** is above every document format in the roster by orders of magnitude — a decompressed
JSON body, an HTML page, a spreadsheet, a CSV export — and below what a request may hold under a
default `[limits] memory`, so a breach is a refusal the request survives rather than an allocation
failure it does not.

**1000:1** is above the ratio of every corpus that is not deliberately compressible. English text
under gzip runs 3:1 to 5:1, minified JavaScript 4:1 to 8:1, a JSON document heavy with repeated keys
20:1 to 60:1, and a log file of near-identical lines reaches into the low hundreds. Published bombs
start around 10^4:1 — they are built from one repeated octet, which is the property a real document
does not have — so the gap between the highest honest ratio and the lowest hostile one is roughly an
order of magnitude in each direction. A number in the middle of that gap refuses no honest input and
admits no bomb.

## Options considered

**A nullable option, `?int $maxLength = null`.** PHP's shape with a Novis type on it. Rejected: the
default value of an optional bound is the whole decision, and `null` spells the wrong one.

**A single absolute ceiling.** Simpler, one number to document. Rejected for § 2's amplification
hole; a per-request budget that every request may reach is not a budget.

**The request's own `[limits] memory`, with no key of its own.** Attractive because it needs no new
configuration: the request already has a heap ceiling, and a bomb exhausts it. Rejected on two
counts. A decompression that reaches the heap ceiling is a *fatal* — the request is stopped and
nothing catches it (`rule:errors/on-limit`) — where a bomb should be a throw the handler answers
with a `400`. And the ratio has no expression in a heap ceiling at all.

**A `Core\Compress\Bound` value object.** A constructor taking both numbers, passed as one argument.
Rejected as surface with no benefit: two `uint` parameters carry the same two numbers, and an object
whose only job is to hold them would need its own reference card, its own registered layout and its
own `.nvst` cases to say what `$maxBytes` already says.

## Decision

### 3. The two keys, and why they are `System`

`[limits] max_decompressed` and `[limits] max_decompression_ratio` are `System`-class
(`rule:config/three-changeability-classes`), sitting inside a block whose own row is `Runtime` — the
arrangement `limits.fatal_reserve_memory` already uses and for its reason. A request may not raise
them, and there is nothing for it to lower: lowering is what the call's own arguments are for, and a
second, request-scoped way to say the same thing would be a second place for it to be wrong.

Neither key is under `[limits.hard]`. That block exists to bound a value a request may set for
itself, and these are not such values: the key *is* the ceiling.

### 4. `false` does not turn it off

`rule:config/three-changeability-classes` gives `false` the meaning "no ceiling" for the limits a
request may raise. These two are read differently and deliberately: an unbounded value, like a
malformed one, reads as the shipped default. The alternative is a configuration file that spells an
unbounded decompression, which is the exact shape this record exists to remove — and one an operator
would write once, for a debugging session, and leave.

### 5. A breach is a throw, and never a truncation

Two things a bound must not do, both of which look reasonable in isolation:

- **Truncate.** Answering the prefix it had reached turns a refusal into a silent wrong answer: the
  caller gets bytes, believes them, and the parser downstream reports a malformed document at
  whatever offset the ceiling fell on.
- **Fail as I/O.** A hostile archive and a full disk are different questions with different
  responses, and a caller that cannot tell them apart retries the one it should refuse.

So a breach is a `ParseError` — the class `Core\Json::decode` already answers for input that did not
match a declared format — carrying a message that names the rule, the half of the bound that
stopped it, and which of the two numbers the caller might legitimately move.

### 6. Both classes, one rule

`Core\Zip` applies this bound per entry *and* across the archive, and gets no second rule of its
own. An archive whose entries are each within the bound and whose total is not is the same attack
one directory level up, and a policy that stopped at the entry would be one an attacker splits.

## Diagnostics

No new diagnostic code: every refusal here is a run-time throw rather than a compile-time refusal,
because the bound is a value and not a shape. What the message owes is in § 5.

## Consequences

**What it spends** (`rule:programs/memory-priority`): the output buffer, bounded by the smaller of
the two numbers and attributable to the request that asked for it. The bound is applied *while* the
output grows — every decoder is driven as a `Read` — so a bomb costs the ceiling and never the size
its header claims. That is the difference between refusing one and surviving one.

**What it costs a program.** A handler decompressing something genuinely large — a 200 MB archive
member — is refused until the operator raises `max_decompressed`. That is the trade the record makes
knowingly: the ceiling is the operator's to raise for the deployment that needs it, and the default
is chosen for the deployment that has not thought about it.

**What it costs the language surface.** Two extra parameters on one member, both defaulted, and two
configuration keys. `Core\Zip` inherits both and adds none.

## Alternatives rejected

Sniffing the declared uncompressed size out of the frame and refusing early. Faster to refuse, and
worthless: the number is attacker-supplied in every format that carries one, so trusting it is
trusting the archive about itself. It is read as a *hint* by no member here.

## Revisiting

If a first-party component ever needs a decompression larger than the ceiling on a path where the
operator cannot be asked — a build tool, not a request — the answer is a capability, not a wider
default, and it is a new record.

## Verification

`crates/nvs-stdlib/src/compress.rs`'s test module holds both halves asserted on both sides of the
boundary, the one-way clamp over both axes, and the three spellings a caller might reach for that do
not mean "unbounded". `tests/conformance/core/compress-refuses-a-decompression-past-its-bound.nvst`
is the same rule from source, including that a bounded-out decompression answers nothing at all.
