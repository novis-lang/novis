# ADR 0176 — A response body may be written over time, and the streaming response ADR 0083 § 5 drew its line against was never built

- **Scope:** the body a response carries while it is being sent, and the two doors onto it — a stream
  that ends with its request, and one that outlives it. It changes nothing about a response this
  server already holds in full, and nothing about the isolate a connection is, which
  [0083](0083.md) decides.
- **Depends on:** [0083](0083.md).
- **Validated by:** `crates/nvs-runtime/src/stream.rs`'s own cases, and
  `crates/nvs-server/src/serve.rs`'s cases over `Answer`'s two arms.

> **In short:** ADR 0083 § 5 separates a *streaming response* from a *connection isolate* by what the
> stream outlives, and calls the first one something "M7 already builds". It never did. Every
> response this server has ever sent is one buffer with an exact length, so § 5's line has stood
> between one thing that did not exist and another, and a reader following it looked for a spelling
> the tree had no word for. The line itself is right and is not what changes: what changes is that
> both sides of it are now built, on one cell, with one chunk in flight and the writer parked until
> the connection has taken it.

## Context

§ 5 is the SSE half of 0083, and it decides the door rather than the isolate: an event stream takes
nothing from the server, so its response is an ordinary `200 text/event-stream` that must still be
sent, and what the connection isolate writes into is that response's body. The section rests that
argument on a distinction it states in two bullets — a stream that ends with its response is a
streaming response, "which M7 already builds", and one that outlives it is a connection isolate.

The first bullet was wrong when it was written and has been wrong since. A response body in this
server is `nvs_server::serve::Answer`, an optional buffer with an exact `Body::size_hint`, which is
what makes `hyper` send a `Content-Length` rather than chunk it. There is no arm for a body that
arrives later, no seam a program could write one through, and nothing that could put a byte on the
wire after the head has gone out. The event-stream cell, the both-cells-filled refusal and the
isolate that starts after the request is joined all landed against that: the door opens onto a
`200 text/event-stream` whose body nothing can write.

A frozen record is not edited when a later decision moves what it rests on, so 0083 keeps its
sentence and this record carries the correction. The rule the distinction became —
`rule:concurrency/a-stream-that-outlives-its-request-is-a-connection` — states a *definition* and
was never a claim about the tree, so it was not false either; what it gains is the sentence saying
both spellings exist, once they do.

## Decision

### 1. Both sides of § 5's line are built here, and the line is unchanged

The correction is the scope, not a revision: a stream that ends with its response and a stream that
outlives it stay two things, distinguished by lifetime and not by protocol, exactly as § 5 and the
rule say. What this record adds is that neither of them was implemented, so both are implemented
together — the request-scoped door and the connection-scoped one, over one shared cell, rather than
the connection-scoped one alone against a streaming response that was assumed already present.

Building only the second would have repeated the original error in the other direction. The event
stream's `send` has to reach a response body somehow; with no body to reach, the door would have
acquired a private writing path that the request-scoped spelling could not use, and the language
would have two mechanisms for what § 5 says is one model with two hand-overs.

### 2. One chunk in flight, and the writer parks until the connection has taken it

The body crosses between two tasks — the isolate that writes it and the connection that frames it —
and `hyper`'s types are polled with the connection's context, so the isolate cannot make the write
side run. That is the seam the request body already crosses in the other direction, and it takes the
same answer: a cell with a wake pair, one chunk held at a time, and the producing side parked while
the cell is full.

Backpressure is therefore structural rather than configured. A program that writes faster than the
peer reads is slowed by the peer, nothing accumulates between them, and there is no queue depth to
choose — which is also what keeps the cost O(in-flight) under
`rule:programs/memory-priority` instead of O(events sent). A writer that could park forever is the
one failure this shape has instead of an unbounded buffer, so the wait is bounded by the
connection's own send timeout (`rule:concurrency/connection-bounds-are-finite`), read from the
connection's bounds rather than named beside them, and a consumer that has gone away closes its
writer at once rather than at the deadline.

## Consequences

`Answer` is two-valued, and every path that answers a request reads it. A whole body keeps its exact
size hint and its `Content-Length` unchanged — the arm is the same buffer it always was, so static
files, refusals and the `304` path are untouched — and a streaming body reports no exact size, which
is what leaves `hyper` chunking it. The cost is at priority 4, simplicity: one more arm in the type
every response passes through, and a server path where the head is answered before the isolate has
ended.

## Verification

`crates/nvs-runtime/src/stream.rs` holds the cell's own cases: the chunk written is the chunk the
consumer takes, a second write parks until the first has been taken, a dropped consumer ends its
writer rather than leaving it parked, and a reader that takes nothing closes the stream at the send
timeout. `crates/nvs-server/src/serve.rs` holds the framing half against a real client on a
loopback socket: a whole body is sent with a `Content-Length`, a streaming body is chunk-framed with
none, `Answer::bytes` still answers the whole arm's buffer, and a stalled reader ends a write as the
bound it met.
