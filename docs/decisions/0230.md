# ADR 0230 — an idle queue worker asks its queue once a second, and a push in the same process wakes it once the job is committed

- **Scope:** how long a `[queue]` worker waits after it finds no due job, and what ends that wait
  early. It does not change the claim, the write-back, the retry ladder or the visibility window of
  [0084](0084.md), it does not change which process runs a job ([0154](0154.md)), and it adds no
  `[queue]` key.
- **Depends on:** [0084](0084.md) (a job is a row, and a push commits with its caller's transaction),
  [0154](0154.md) (the workers run inside `nvs serve`, and the drain stops them), [0229](0229.md) (a
  task may take a wake handle for one wait and let it go).
- **Validated by:** the cases under *Verification* in `crates/nvs-cli/src/worker.rs`,
  `crates/nvs-stdlib/src/db/transaction.rs`, `crates/nvs-stdlib/src/queue/bell.rs` and
  `crates/nvs-runtime/src/ctx/held.rs`, and the measurement there.

> **In short:** a worker that finds no due job waits one second before it asks the database again.
> It asks at once when it starts and at once after each job. `Core\Queue::push` in the same process
> wakes the idle workers once the job is committed, so that job starts without waiting for the poll.
> A stop wakes them too, under `nvs run` and under `nvs serve`. The wake is a call the process makes,
> so every database driver takes the same path and none of them is asked for a notification channel.
> The price: a job pushed by another process, a delayed job, a retry and a job reclaimed after its
> worker died may each start up to one second late.

## Context

A worker that found no due job waited ten milliseconds and asked again. No record and no rule chose
that figure. It came with the first worker, which ran beside a script under `nvs run`, where the wait
is what a program polling for its own job waits on. `nvs serve` took the same worker over and ran it
for as long as the server is up.

So a server with a `[queue]` and nothing in it sent about a hundred roster statements per second for
each worker, all day. The same server with no `[queue]` does no work at all while it is idle
([0229](0229.md)).

A longer wait fixes the idle cost and breaks the case the short wait was for: a request pushes a job
and expects it to start now. Both hold at once only when the push tells the workers.

## Investigation

Three questions decided whether the design could be built as one path.

**Can a push on one core reach workers on another?** Under `nvs serve` the workers are armed on the
one core that ticks, and a request runs on whichever core accepted it. The reactor's cross-thread wake
is `Send` and is how the drain, begun on the thread that took the signal, wakes waits parked on every
core. A push takes the same route.

**Is there a place to act after a commit?** `Core\Db` had no hook for it. It has one function that
closes every level a `transaction` opens, on every driver, and that function knows the depth before
its own `BEGIN`. That is enough to tell the outermost commit from a nested one and from a rollback.

**What depended on the ten milliseconds?** The queue examples push from the process that runs the
workers, so the wake covers them. No test asserted the interval itself.

## Decision

### 1. One fixed wait of one second

A worker claims when it starts and again after each job it writes back. A worker whose claim found
nothing waits one second. There is no backoff and no `[queue]` key for the wait.

### 2. A push in the same process wakes the idle workers

The process has one bell. A worker with nothing to claim registers a wake on it for the length of its
wait, and `Core\Queue::push` rings it. A ring wakes every idle worker, and each asks the database at
once.

The bell counts its rings. A worker reads the count before it asks for work, and registers against
that reading. A ring in between refuses the registration, and the worker asks again. Without the
count, a push that committed just after a worker's claim looked, and rang just before the worker
registered, would leave that job to the one-second wait.

### 3. The wake follows the commit

A worker woken before the job is committed asks for a row no other connection can see, finds
nothing, and waits again. So the ring is tied to the commit:

- A push outside a transaction is its own committed statement. It rings when the statement has run.
- A push inside a `transaction` on the queue's own connection rings when that transaction commits.
  When transactions nest, it rings at the outermost commit. A rollback rings nothing.
- A push while a transaction is open on a different connection commits on its own, so it rings when
  the statement has run.

`push` reads how many transaction levels are open on the queue's connection once its insert has run.
At zero it rings. Otherwise it records the level on the request's entry for that connection, and
`transaction` reports every level it closes. One number per connection is kept, the shallowest level
an enqueue is recorded under. A rollback clears a record made at its level or deeper, a nested commit
hands the record to the level around it, and the outermost commit rings.

A transaction a program opens in its own statement text is not seen by the runtime. A push inside one
rings at the statement, the woken workers find nothing, and the job is found at the next poll.

### 4. A stop wakes the workers

The switch that stops a run's workers rings the bell after it sets its flag. That is the script's
exit under `nvs run`, and a reload that takes a worker away under `nvs serve`. A served worker also
registers on the drain for the length of its wait. A woken worker reads the stop at the top of its
next turn and returns without asking the database again.

### 5. One path for every driver

The ring is a call the process makes after its own statement or its own commit. No driver sends or
receives anything for it. PostgreSQL's `LISTEN` and `NOTIFY` are not used, and neither is any other
backend's notification channel.

## Consequences

- **Performance.** An idle `[queue]` sends one roster statement per worker per second, where it sent
  about a hundred. A push costs one uncontended lock, and one cross-thread wake for each idle worker.
  A ring wakes every idle worker, so one push may cost each of them a roster statement that finds
  nothing left.
- **Memory.** One word per database connection a request has open, and one boxed wake per idle worker
  for as long as it waits. Nothing grows with requests served or jobs pushed.
- **Usability.** A job pushed by the same process starts as before. A job pushed by another process,
  a delayed job, a retry and a job reclaimed after its worker died may each start up to one second
  late, where the bound was ten milliseconds. A stop and a script's exit do not wait out the second.
- **Simplicity.** Nothing new for a program to write and no new setting. The implementation gains the
  bell, one number on a request's connection entry, and one call in `transaction` for each level it
  closes. The idle wait no longer bounds a shutdown, because a stop ends it.

## Alternatives rejected

- **`LISTEN` and `NOTIFY` on PostgreSQL, and the nearest thing on each other backend.** It would
  cover a push from another process. It is one mechanism per driver, SQLite has none, and each one is
  a second connection state to keep correct. The one-second bound covers the same case on every
  backend.
- **A backoff that grows while the queue stays empty.** A job's delay would depend on how long the
  queue had been idle, which an operator cannot read off anything.
- **A `[queue]` key for the interval.** With the wake in place the interval only bounds work this
  process was not told about. A key would be one more number to choose for a case a second serves.
- **Ring at the statement inside a transaction.** The workers would ask before the row is visible,
  find nothing, and the job would wait out the second: the exact case the wake exists for.
- **Wake one worker per push.** A transaction that pushes many jobs rings once at its commit, and one
  woken worker would run them one after another while the rest waited out the second.
- **Shorten the wait and keep polling.** Any interval short enough to feel immediate is a poll an idle
  server pays all day.

## Verification

Counted, and not timed, wherever a count answers the question ([0228](0228.md)).

In `crates/nvs-cli/src/worker.rs`, each against a real worker on a SQLite file:

- `an_idle_worker_asks_once_and_then_once_per_idle_wait` counts the turns an idle worker takes in a
  window and holds them to one, plus one for each whole second the window really took.
- `a_runs_exit_wakes_an_idle_worker_and_it_returns_without_asking_again` and
  `a_drain_wakes_an_idle_worker_and_it_returns_without_asking_again` give the worker an idle wait of an
  hour, stop it while it waits, and count one turn. A run that is not woken does not end, and fails.
- `a_ring_ends_an_idle_workers_wait_and_the_job_it_announced_runs` enqueues a job while the worker
  waits out an hour, rings, and reads the job back as succeeded, claimed on the worker's second turn.
- `a_job_nothing_announced_is_claimed_when_the_idle_wait_ends` enqueues a job with no ring, which is
  what a push from another process is to this one, and reads it back as succeeded.

In `crates/nvs-stdlib/src/db/transaction.rs`,
`a_push_rings_after_the_commit_and_a_rollback_rings_nothing` runs `transaction` on a real SQLite
connection and counts the process bell's rings inside the transaction and after it: outside a
transaction, committed, rolled back, nested, and nested and rolled back. The cases in
`crates/nvs-runtime/src/ctx/held.rs` hold the level bookkeeping, and the cases in
`crates/nvs-stdlib/src/queue/bell.rs` hold the bell.

One `nvs serve` of a one-line program with `[queue]` on a SQLite file, `workers = 2` and no jobs,
beside the same server with no `[queue]`. Release builds, one machine, all four servers inside the
same thirty idle seconds, two runs. Cycles are what `QueryProcessCycleTime` counts. The charged time
is what Windows adds at its clock interrupt, in whole ticks, and is the coarser reading.

| Server | Cycles in thirty seconds | Charged CPU time |
|---|---|---|
| before, `[queue]` with two workers | 1 595 million, 1 571 million | 391 ms, 391 ms |
| after, `[queue]` with two workers | 48 million, 54 million | 16 ms, 0 ms |
| before, no `[queue]` | 18 million, 19 million | 0 ms, 0 ms |
| after, no `[queue]` | 21 million, 20 million | 0 ms, 0 ms |
