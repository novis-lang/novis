# ADR 0156 — a body reader is buffering or streaming, and only a streaming one consumes the body

- **Scope:** which readers of `Core\Request`'s body may follow which, and why that is a property of the
  member rather than a list of pairs. It does not decide `json()`'s signature or its error behaviour,
  which are [0097](0097.md) § 8's roster and this goal's own standing decisions.
- **Depends on:** [0097](0097.md) § 8 (the body, the two caps and the streaming reader),
  [0105](0105.md) § 2 (a multipart form's non-file parts are buffered into `post()`),
  [0095](0095.md) (ambiguous input is refused whole).
- **Validated by:** the refusal `crates/nvs-stdlib/src/request.rs`'s `claim_body` states to a program,
  and the conformance cases that pin a second buffering read answering the same octets.

> **In short:** every member that reads a request body either **keeps** what it read or **consumes** it.
> The buffering ones — `body`, `post`, `json`, `jsonAs` — fill the request's hold under
> `[limits] request_body` and answer out of it, so any of them may follow any other. The streaming ones —
> `bodyStream`, `files` — hand the octets to the program and keep none, so each may only be the first
> reader. `post()` following a `files()` walk stops being a carve-out: the walk buffers the non-file
> fields, and `post()` reads a hold like every other buffering reader.

## Context

Spec § 15 and `rule:http-server/the-body-is-read-on-demand-under-two-caps` stated the old rule as a set:
`body`, `bodyStream` and `files` are mutually exclusive on one request, because each consumes the stream
the other two would read. One member sat outside the set — `post()`, which reads the fields a `files()`
walk buffers — and the exclusivity sentence carried it as a named exception.

Goal `request-json` adds `json()` and `jsonAs<T>()`. Both read the whole body, both would be perfectly safe after
`body()` and before `post()`, and neither consumes anything: they decode from octets that are already
held. Adding them to the exclusive set would refuse programs that are plainly correct — a handler that
logs `body()` and then decodes `json()` is not a defect. Exempting them alongside `post()` would put two
named exceptions beside a rule of three, which is a second rule nobody wrote down.

The old set was also stated over the wrong fact. Exclusivity was never about which members were named
together; it was about whether the octets survive the call. Three members happened to be the ones that
did not, at a moment when three were all there were.

## Options considered

1. **Add `json` and `jsonAs` to the exclusive set.** Cheapest edit, and wrong: `json()` after `body()`
   would throw where nothing was consumed, and every future reader repeats the argument.
2. **Add them as further exceptions, beside `post()`.** Three exceptions to a four-member set is not a
   rule; it is a table, and a table has to be maintained by whoever adds the fifth member.
3. **Classify the members by what they do with the octets.** The runtime already knows this — a member
   either filled the hold or did not. The classification is derivable rather than declared, so a member
   added later answers the question by construction. **Chosen.**

## Decision

### 1. Two kinds, and the kind decides what may follow

A **buffering** reader reads the body into the request's hold, bounded by `[limits] request_body`, and
answers out of it. A **streaming** reader hands the octets to the program as they arrive and retains
none of them. A buffering reader may follow any other buffering reader, in any order, and each answers
the same octets; a streaming reader may only be the first reader, and after it nothing reads those
octets again.

Today that makes `body`, `post`, `json` and `jsonAs` buffering, and `bodyStream` and `files` streaming.
The rule is not that list: the list is what the rule currently classifies.

### 2. `post()` after `files()` is the rule, not an exception to it

A `files()` walk is streaming for the file parts and buffering for everything else — [0105](0105.md) § 2
already requires the non-file fields to be buffered so that an ordinary `<form>` with a title and a file
reads the way it was written. Those buffered fields are the hold `post()` answers out of, and they are
charged against `request_body` exactly as [0105](0105.md) § 2 says. So `post()` after a walk is a
buffering reader reading a hold, which is what buffering readers do. `body()` after a walk is still
refused, because the hold carries the decoded fields and never the raw octets.

`post()` drains whatever the walk did not reach before it answers. That is unchanged from spec § 15 and
is what makes it report every field rather than the ones that arrived ahead of the part the walk stopped
on; a handler that wants the uploads takes `files()` first.

### 3. What a buffering reader keeps beyond the octets

`json()` keeps the `Value` it decoded, because `Core\Json::decode` produces only arrays and scalars and
an array is copy-on-write, so a second caller can be handed a refcount bump. `jsonAs<T>()` keeps nothing
beyond the octets and decodes per call, because `decodeAs` builds objects and two callers must never be
handed the same one. The memory this spends is stated in the rule under
`rule:programs/memory-priority`: at most `request_body` of held octets per in-flight request, which was
already `post()`'s bill, plus one decoded value for a request that called `json()`, both freed with the
request.

## Diagnostics

No new code. A reader arriving after the body was consumed throws `LogicError`, as `claim_body` does
today, and its message names the member that consumed it. What changes is which pairs reach the throw
and what the message says: the old text cites spec § 15's three-way exclusivity and `post()`'s carve-out,
and the replacement says that a streaming reader consumed the body.

## Consequences

- `body()` after `post()`, and `json()` after either, stop throwing. No program that worked before stops
  working: the change only removes refusals.
- `Inbound` grows a general hold — `hold_body`, generalizing `hold_form` — so the octets a buffering
  reader read outlive its call. The footprint is unchanged in the worst case, because a `post()` of the
  same body already held them.
- A future body reader is placed by asking one question about it, and the rule needs no edit to admit it.

## Alternatives rejected

- **Re-reading the stream for a second buffering reader** — impossible for a body already off the wire,
  and answering empty instead is the repair `rule:errors/ambiguous-input-refused` forbids: an empty
  answer from an exhausted stream is indistinguishable from a body that was empty.
- **Letting `bodyStream()` follow `body()` by replaying the hold.** It would be honest for a body under
  `request_body`, and it would make the member mean two different things depending on what ran before it
  — bounded by the connection's idle timeout in one case and by nothing in the other. A streaming reader
  reads the wire or it is refused.
- **Gating the classification on `Content-Type`.** Rejected on `post()`'s own reasoning: a mislabelled
  but valid document is read, and the kind of a reader is a property of the member, not of the request.

## Verification

`crates/nvs-stdlib/src/request.rs`'s claim path, and conformance cases under `tests/conformance/core/`
that read one body twice through two buffering members and assert both answers, and that a buffering
member after `bodyStream()` throws `LogicError` naming it.
